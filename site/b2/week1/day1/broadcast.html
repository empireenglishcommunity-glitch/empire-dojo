Lecturer
Good morning. Today I want to talk about why progress stops, and I want to be careful about a distinction that most people collapse. Consider two students. The first has studied English for six years. The second has been studying English for six years. In everyday speech we treat those as the same sentence. They are not. The first tells you about a completed quantity: six years exist, they are in the past, and something resulted. The second tells you about an unfinished activity, and it carries an implication the first does not, which is that the effort is still going on, and possibly going on longer than expected. Now, here is why this matters beyond grammar. We have known since the nineteen sixties that skill acquisition is not linear. What we have been discovering more recently is less comfortable: the plateau is not a failure of effort. Learners on a plateau have usually been working harder than they were at the start. They have been repeating what they already do well, because repeating what you do well feels like progress and feels safe. So the question to ask is not 'how long have you been studying?' It is 'what have you actually completed?' The first question measures your patience. The second measures your progress. Most of us have been answering the first one for years, and calling it the second.
