📄 Transcript / النص

Now read while you listen again — check what you missed. / اقرا وإنت بتسمع تاني — شوف إيه اللي فاتك.

Host
Every guest on this programme this month has told me what most people want. Dalia, you actually measure it.
Dalia
And that is why I almost never use the phrase. 'Most people' is doing something dishonest in nearly every sentence it appears in. Take last week's figure: sixty-one per cent support the scheme. That is most people, in a sense. But of those sixty-one, fewer than half could describe what the scheme does, and a third said they would oppose it if it raised their own costs, which it will. So 'most people support it' is both true and useless.
Minister
With respect, all of politics works like that. Neither of us has a better instrument. Every mandate in every parliament rests on a number that thin.
Dalia
I agree, and that is not a defence. It is the problem. The honest sentence is longer: a majority express support, few understand the detail, and support falls sharply when costs are mentioned. Nobody puts that on a poster.
Minister
Because a poster with that on it loses.
Host
So is there any question where the answer is stable?
Dalia
Yes. Almost all of them, if you ask twice, six months apart, and count only the people who say the same thing both times. That group is usually about a quarter. Every serious campaign is fought over the other three quarters, and each of them will tell you that they are most people.
