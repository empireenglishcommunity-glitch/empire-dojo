As It Turned Out

وكما تبيّن لاحقًا

سرد لقصة مصنع: القرار اللي بان تافه في وقته هو اللي حدّد كل حاجة بعدها.

📄 Read / اقرا

In the spring of 2008 the factory was going to close.

Everyone involved understood this, and the only question was the order of events.

As it turned out, the order was the whole story.

The closure had been due to take effect in September.

Prior to that, in June, the company was to have transferred its two largest contracts to a plant three hundred kilometres away.

Little did anyone know that the receiving plant would itself be on the point of a labour dispute, which by the time the transfer was scheduled had escalated to the extent that nothing could be moved.

The factory was therefore kept open, at first for six weeks, thereafter indefinitely.

Meanwhile the workforce, which had been told in March that it would later be offered relocation, had begun to leave.

Forty per cent had gone by August.

What was destined to be a managed wind-down became, without anyone deciding it, an understaffed continuation, and the plant that was to become a distribution depot subsequently spent four years as neither.

In retrospect the turning point is easy to identify, and it is not the labour dispute.

It is a decision taken in April, which at the outset looked purely administrative: the company chose not to announce the closure date to customers until the transfer was complete.

Because customers were not told, orders continued to arrive.

Because orders arrived, the six-week extension was commercially defensible.

Because it was defensible, it was granted, and it was never seriously revisited.

The site is profitable today.

Its survival is regularly attributed to loyalty, and occasionally to foresight.

Neither is quite it.

It was bound to close, and it did not, because a communications decision made in April was allowed to transpire into a strategy nobody had chosen.
