A Can of Worms

فتح باب لا يُغلق

نص عن استخدام التعبيرات المجازية: المجاز بيوقف التفكير بدل ما يشرحه.

📄 Read / اقرا

The minutes record that the committee agreed the matter was "a grey area" and resolved not to "open a can of worms".

Both phrases are doing something more specific than they appear to, and it is worth getting to the bottom of what.

A grey area, taken literally, is a region where a rule does not determine an outcome.

Used figuratively, and it is nearly always used figuratively, it more often describes a region where the rule is perfectly clear and applying it would be costly.

The connotation is of intellectual difficulty.

The function is permission to stop.

That is a double-edged sword: the phrase is genuinely useful where ambiguity is real, and it is precisely because it is useful that it works so well when it is not.

"A can of worms" is more candid, in a way.

It concedes that further enquiry would find something.

The undertone is not that the question is hard but that the answer would be inconvenient, and the metaphor is chosen — one may infer — by people who would rather not be quoted saying so.

By and large this is how such expressions earn their place.

They evoke a shared attitude while committing nobody to a proposition.

In the long run they also shape what a committee is able to notice, since a matter filed as grey is not filed as unresolved, and only one of those two categories generates a review date.

None of which is an argument for banning idiom, an allusion-free prose being both unreadable and no more honest.

It is an argument for reading the minutes figuratively.

What was actually decided here was not to look, and the phrase that shed light on it is the one nobody drafted carefully.

drafted carefully.
