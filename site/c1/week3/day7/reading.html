What Actually Matters

اللي يهم فعلًا

مقال عن تقييم المدارس: الكاتب بيقول إن اللي بيتقاس مش هو اللي مهم.

📄 Read / اقرا

It is not the size of a school's budget that predicts what its pupils learn.

That much is well established.

What is less often said, and what this article sets out to underline, is that we know which factor does predict it and have chosen to measure something else.

The crux is teacher retention.

Schools that keep their staff for five years outperform comparable schools that do not, and the gap is decisive rather than subtle.

It is precisely this that our inspection framework fails to capture.

What the framework prioritises is documentation: lesson plans, marking policies, evidence of intervention.

All of these are visible in a single day's visit, which is, one suspects, the actual criterion.

Above all, it is worth noting what the published rankings reward.

A school can rise two bands by improving its paperwork while losing a third of its teachers, and several have.

Little wonder that headteachers pinpoint compliance rather than retention as their priority.

They are not confused about what matters.

They are responding accurately to what is counted.

There is a striking asymmetry here.

The significance of retention is acknowledged in every policy document; the aspect of school life it depends on — whether teaching there is bearable — appears in none of them.

Namely: workload, autonomy, and whether a complaint from a parent is investigated or simply forwarded.

The essence of the problem is therefore not measurement error.

It is that we have a metric which is cheap, defensible and nearly irrelevant, and another which is expensive, contestable and nearly sufficient.

We have made the choice any institution makes, and we describe it as rigour.

describe it as rigour.
