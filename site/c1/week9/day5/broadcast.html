Seminar lead
An argument fails at its joints far more often than at its premises, and that is worth stating plainly insofar as most training in critical reading concentrates on evidence, whereas the errors surviving peer review are almost invariably errors of connection. Consider a structure common in policy writing. A premise is established, evidence is presented, and a conclusion follows introduced by 'thus' or 'hence' or, more ambitiously, 'accordingly'. The evidence may be impeccable. Nevertheless the conjunction is doing work that has not been earned, in that it asserts a relation the data do not contain: correlation is reported and causation inferred, whereby a marker becomes a mechanism without anybody having claimed that it is one. Conversely, a genuinely cohesive argument can be recognised by a simple test. Remove every discourse marker and read what remains. If the sequence still compels, the markers were describing a structure that was there; if it collapses into a list, they were supplying one that was not. Coherence is a property of the reasoning, cohesion a property of the prose, and the second is regularly mistaken for the first. Notwithstanding this, 'moreover' is not a crime; signposting is what allows a reader to follow a long chain at all, and papers stripped of it are measurably harder to use. The rationale for attending to joints is practical. Evidence can be checked by anyone with the data; a connective cannot, which is why it is where a weak case is most safely concealed, and why the reviewer who concedes the evidence and interrogates the conjunction is the one who tends to refute something. And I'd add that this is learnable in an afternoon, which is why I find its absence from most methods training genuinely difficult to account for.
