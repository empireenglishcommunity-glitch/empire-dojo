What is the comedian's underlying point about correcting people's grammar?

That most grammatical rules were invented in the last century
That formal linguistic training should be compulsory in schools
That his mother was right about usage all along
That correcting others is usually about power and identity rather than being understood

❓ Question / سؤال 1

Why does he say 'you can't correct what you didn't understand'?

Because it proves correction is not aimed at clarity — the meaning already arrived
Because his father spoke a different dialect at home
Because children's speech is often genuinely unintelligible
Because the correction always comes too late to help

❓ Question / سؤال 2

What does he mean by calling a grammar preference 'a costume'?

It is something learned only in formal education
It changes depending on who you are speaking to
It is a display signalling what kind of person you are
It is easily removed once pointed out

❓ Question / سؤال 3

What does the story about the work-experience kid do in the set?

It shows the comedian was unfairly blamed for someone else's remark
It proves his degree finally became useful at work
It explains why he left the job
It turns the joke back on himself and reveals he inherited the behaviour

❓ Question / سؤال 4

Why does he single out 'ten items or less'?

Because it is an example of an error nobody is actually confused by
Because supermarkets have since corrected the signs
Because it is the one error that genuinely causes confusion
Because his mother used to say it

📄 Transcript / النص

Now read while you listen again — check what you missed. / اقرا وإنت بتسمع تاني — شوف إيه اللي فاتك.

Comedian
So I have a degree in linguistics, which is a qualification that is useful in exactly one situation, and that situation is an argument with your mother. Genuinely. Genuinely the only application. Four years. Fourteen thousand pounds. So that when she says 'nobody says it like that' I can say 'nine hundred years of attested usage says it like that, Mum,' and she can say 'well, they're wrong as well.' And she wins. She wins because she's my mother, and because 'they're wrong as well' is unfalsifiable, which — yes, thank you, sir, one person. One. That's my crowd. That's my people. That man is going home to nobody. ... The thing is, everyone thinks they hate grammar and everyone is a fanatic about it. Everyone. You've all got one. You've got one thing that if somebody does it in an email you no longer respect them as a person. Don't shake your head, madam, you've got two. I can see it from here. And here's what kills me: the ones people get furious about are never the ones that stop you understanding the sentence. Nobody's ever been genuinely confused by 'less' instead of 'fewer'. Nobody's stood in a supermarket going 'ten items or less, well, now I simply do not know how many tins I may purchase.' It isn't confusion. It's a costume. It's a little flag you run up so people know which sort of person you are. My dad used to correct me at dinner. Every night. Not to teach me anything — he'd already understood me, that's the point, you can't correct what you didn't understand. He corrected me because he was thirty years into a job where nobody let him finish a sentence, and I was eleven, and I was available. And I did the same thing. I did the exact same thing to a work-experience kid three years ago, in front of people, over an apostrophe, and I watched his face do the thing my face used to do. And I thought: ah. There it is. That's what it's for. Fourteen thousand pounds to find out my degree is a weapon my dad handed me and I picked up. ... Anyway. Great to be in Manchester.
