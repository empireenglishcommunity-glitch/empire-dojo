The Myth of the Finished Project

أسطورة المشروع اللي خلص
B2.R.3

199 words

We have been measuring the wrong thing. Organisations celebrate the achievement they can announce, not the ongoing effort that produced it, and the result is a culture that rewards endings.

Consider a team that has been working on the same system for two years. Lately they have improved reliability by a third, but there is nothing to launch, so their progress is invisible. Meanwhile a second team accomplishes a small, temporary fix, announces it before the deadline, and receives the credit. So far, so familiar.

My own view is that this is not merely unfair; it is expensive. When people learn that only completed targets are counted, they stop focusing on the work that cannot be finished — maintenance, documentation, the slow development of skill. Those are precisely the activities that decide whether an organisation still functions in five years.

Some managers reply that you cannot reward what you cannot measure. That is true, but it is an argument for better measurement, not for none. Progress can be described even when it cannot be photographed. Until we learn to say 'this has been improving' as confidently as we say 'this is done', we will keep paying for the difference.

Lecturerbf_isabella
Good morning. Today I want to talk about why progress stops, and I want to be careful about a distinction that most people collapse. Consider two students. The first has studied English for six years. The second has been studying English for six years. In everyday speech we treat those as the same sentence. They are not. The first tells you about a completed quantity: six years exist, they are in the past, and something resulted. The second tells you about an unfinished activity, and it carries an implication the first does not, which is that the effort is still going on, and possibly going on longer than expected. Now, here is why this matters beyond grammar. We have known since the nineteen sixties that skill acquisition is not linear. What we have been discovering more recently is less comfortable: the plateau is not a failure of effort. Learners on a plateau have usually been working harder than they were at the start. They have been repeating what they already do well, because repeating what you do well feels like progress and feels safe. So the question to ask is not 'how long have you been studying?' It is 'what have you actually completed?' The first question measures your patience. The second measures your progress. Most of us have been answering the first one for years, and calling it the second.

The Cost of Hindsight

تكلفة إدراك الأمور متأخّر
B2.R.3

187 words

If you had known then what you know now, you would have decided differently. That sentence is true of almost every decision, which is exactly why it explains almost nothing.

Hindsight creates a consequence we rarely notice: it makes past decisions look obvious. Had the outcome been different, we would have called the same choice reckless. A manager who invested in a project that failed is blamed for not having foreseen it; if it had succeeded, nobody would have asked what she had risked.

This matters because organisations use the past to train the future. If we only examine decisions whose outcomes were bad, we learn to avoid the wrong things. We conclude that the mistake was the investment, when in fact the mistake may have been the absence of a plan for failure.

A better question than 'what should they have done?' is 'what could they have known?' Had the warning signs been available and ignored, that is a real error. If they were not available, no amount of regret would have prevented the outcome — and treating it as a lesson teaches a false one.

The Historian's Temptation

B2.R.1

Before you listen: A lecture defending a kind of sentence that historians officially distrust. Listen for the CONDITION she attaches to her defence.

Lectureraf_bella
Historians are trained to distrust a certain kind of sentence, and I want to defend it anyway. The sentence is: if that had not happened, we would not be here. Notice the shape of it. The condition is in the past; the consequence is in the present. If the treaty had been signed in eighteen seventy, this region would be a single country today. If she had not missed that train, you would not exist. These are mixed conditionals, and they are the natural grammar of historical explanation, which is why historians use them constantly while officially disapproving of them. The objection is reasonable. We cannot test them. If the treaty had been signed, a thousand other things would also have changed, and we cannot hold those still while we vary the one we are interested in. So the sentence looks like an argument and behaves like a story. But consider the alternative. If we refuse all counterfactuals, we lose the ability to say that anything mattered. To say that a decision was important is to say that the present would be different without it. There is no other content to the word 'important'. A history with no counterfactuals is not a more rigorous history; it is a chronicle, a list of things that happened in order, and it cannot distinguish the treaty from the weather that afternoon. So: use them, and say out loud that you are using them. The danger is not the sentence. The danger is the sentence pretending to be a fact.

Although, However, Nevertheless

لكن، ومع ذلك، وبالرغم من كده
B2.R.1

Before you listen: A lecture made in four announced moves, one of which is a concession. Listen for the CONCLUSION and how he signals it.

Lecturerbm_lewis
I want to make an argument in four moves, and I want to signal each move as I make it, because the signalling is the lesson. First, the claim. Discourse markers are not decoration. 'However' is not a more formal 'but'. It performs a different act: it tells the listener that what follows will not merely differ from what preceded, but will outweigh it. Second, the evidence. When we removed the markers from recorded lectures and played them to students, comprehension of the individual sentences was unaffected. Recall of the argument's direction fell by roughly forty per cent. The students could tell you what had been said. They could not tell you which side the speaker was on. Third, and this is the concession, which I am flagging deliberately, the effect is smaller in writing. Readers can go back. Listeners cannot. So it is possible to write reasonably well while using markers carelessly, which is why many fluent writers are poor lecturers. Nevertheless, and here is the fourth move, the conclusion is stronger than the concession. If your listener cannot reconstruct the shape of your argument, you have not made an argument; you have produced a sequence of true sentences. And notice what I did just then. I said 'nevertheless', and you knew, before you heard any of the content, that the concession was about to be overruled. That is the whole mechanism, and it cost me one word.

What Matters Is the Emphasis

اللي يهم هو التوكيد
B2.R.3

188 words

It is not the facts that persuade people; it is the order they arrive in. What most writers underestimate is how much emphasis decides.

Consider a sentence carrying three pieces of information: the delay, the cause and the remedy. Put the remedy last and the reader remembers a problem. Put it first and they remember a plan. The facts are identical; what changes is which one the sentence points at. That is precisely why 'the reason we cancelled was the weather' lands so differently from 'the weather cancelled it'.

What matters particularly is the end of a sentence. English gives its final position enormous weight, so a crucial detail buried in the middle is, in fact, hidden. Writers who are so worried about being unclear that they explain everything twice often bury the essential point in the second explanation.

The thing is, emphasis cannot be added afterwards with bold type. It is built by structure. If you want a reader to leave with one idea, the honest test is simple: read the last six words of each paragraph and ask whether they are the six you would choose.

The Register You Did Not Choose

النبرة اللي مااخترتهاش
B2.R.3

191 words

Every message carries two messages: what you said, and how close you assumed you were to the reader. Most misunderstandings I have had to sort out at work came from the second one.

A colleague writes 'can you look into this?' and means it warmly. Read by somebody who was expecting 'would you mind looking into this?', it arrives as an instruction. Neither person has done anything wrong; they have simply set up different assumptions about distance. When we then try to deal with the misunderstanding, we usually discuss the task, which was never the problem.

This is why I would turn down the advice to 'just be natural' in a second language. Natural in which relationship? A learner who has picked up phrasal verbs from films may come up with something perfectly fluent and far too informal for the email they are writing.

The skill worth taking on is not formality. It is range: being able to say the same thing three ways and to notice which one the situation is asking for. Pay attention to that, and you will call off far fewer arguments you never meant to start.
