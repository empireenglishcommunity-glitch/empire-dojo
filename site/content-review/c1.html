Rarely does a training programme survive its first year unchanged, and seldom does one survive it twice. What follows is not a triumph narrative, although it has been sold as one.

The clinic opened with fourteen staff and a daunting brief: to treat, within eighteen months, a population that had never had access to physiotherapy. No sooner had the doors opened than the first setback arrived. The equipment, delayed at the port, did not clear customs for eleven weeks. Not only did the staff have no machines; they had no protocol for working without them.

What they did next is described, in the funder's report, as remarkable resilience. It was certainly perseverance. Whether it was good practice is a separate question, and one the report under no circumstances raises. Staff improvised, using furniture and their own body weight, and they persisted for nearly three months. Two of them developed the injuries they had been hired to treat.

Hardly anyone reading the published account would guess that. The word "adversity" appears four times. The word "injury" does not appear at all. The relentless emphasis on grit obscures a simpler finding: the obstacle was administrative, it was foreseeable, and it had nothing to do with anybody's character.

There is a version of this story in which fourteen people overcame adversity against all expectations. There is another in which they endured an avoidable failure of logistics and were then congratulated for enduring it. Both are accurate. Only one raises money.

The clinic now treats two hundred patients a week. It also keeps a customs officer on retainer, which is what actually solved the problem, and which nobody has called extraordinary.

What Actually Matters

It is not the size of a school's budget that predicts what its pupils learn. That much is well established. What is less often said, and what this article sets out to underline, is that we know which factor does predict it and have chosen to measure something else.

The crux is teacher retention. Schools that keep their staff for five years outperform comparable schools that do not, and the gap is decisive rather than subtle. It is precisely this that our inspection framework fails to capture. What the framework prioritises is documentation: lesson plans, marking policies, evidence of intervention. All of these are visible in a single day's visit, which is, one suspects, the actual criterion.

Above all, it is worth noting what the published rankings reward. A school can rise two bands by improving its paperwork while losing a third of its teachers, and several have. Little wonder that headteachers pinpoint compliance rather than retention as their priority. They are not confused about what matters. They are responding accurately to what is counted.

There is a striking asymmetry here. The significance of retention is acknowledged in every policy document; the aspect of school life it depends on — whether teaching there is bearable — appears in none of them. Namely: workload, autonomy, and whether a complaint from a parent is investigated or simply forwarded.

The essence of the problem is therefore not measurement error. It is that we have a metric which is cheap, defensible and nearly irrelevant, and another which is expensive, contestable and nearly sufficient. We have made the choice any institution makes, and we describe it as rigour.

It is widely believed that the reservoir project reduced regional water stress by a third. It is also claimed, in the same paragraph of the same briefing, that the figure is provisional. Both statements are unattributed, and the difference between them is the subject of this note.

Consider what the impersonal construction does. When it is reported that consumption fell, the reader is not told who reported it, how, or whether anyone disagreed. The claim is thought to be credible because it is presented as though already settled. It is estimated that the reduction was 31 per cent — but estimated by whom, and against which baseline? The 2011 figure is generally used; it is also known to have been collected during a drought, which makes any later year look like an improvement.

The authorities are said to have commissioned an independent review. They are also reported, by two of the three engineers involved, to have declined to publish it. Note that both sentences are equally passive and equally unverifiable, and that only one of them is likely to be repeated.

None of this is an allegation of bad faith. The construction is standard in official writing, and it is often used simply to avoid a clumsy attribution. But it has a systematic effect: it converts contested findings into consensus. Where a named author must defend a hypothesis, "it is argued" needs no defence at all, because nobody is arguing.

The reader's practical test is short. If a claim cannot be traced to somebody who could be wrong, it has not yet been substantiated — however widely it is believed, and however often it is allegedly disputed by people the text will not name.

You Needn't Have

C1.R.3

A manager who says "you might as well finish it today" has not given an instruction, and both parties usually know it. The distinction matters, because a great deal of workplace friction is a disagreement about modality rather than about facts.

Consider four sentences an employee may hear. "You ought to have flagged this" is a reproach about something no longer changeable. "You needn't have stayed late" may well be gratitude, or it may be a criticism of judgement, and only tone separates them. "You'd better speak to Legal" presents itself as advice while carrying an implied consequence. And "you're supposed to log it within a day" is the only one of the four that states an actual obligation, though it is the mildest in form.

People are bound to read these inconsistently. An employee who is inclined to caution treats every one of them as obligatory; one who is predisposed to autonomy treats all four as advisable and no more. Neither is being unreasonable. The necessity is genuinely unstated, and where it is unstated the reader supplies it from temperament.

Managers reluctant to give direct instructions often believe they are extending discretion. Frequently they are transferring risk. If the employee is not entitled to know which of the four they are hearing, they cannot be held liable for choosing wrongly, and yet invariably they are.

The remedy is unglamorous and it is not a policy. It is a habit: say whether a thing is required, advisable, or merely available, and accept the constraint that this places on you. A manager who cannot bear to be that explicit is not granting freedom. They are declining to decide, and calling it trust.

It concludes that the scheme "appears to have delivered benefits to some extent", that costs were "relatively contained", and that the approach is "arguably suitable for wider adoption, subject to further work". Each of those is defensible. Together they are something else.

Hedging has a legitimate function. Where evidence is genuinely tentative, "the data tend to suggest" is more honest than a flat assertion, and a good analyst would be right to qualify a finding that is only apparent. On the whole, our professional literature would be improved by rather more of this and rather less confidence.

That is not what is happening here. Notice which claims are hedged and which are not. The benefits, which are contested, appear with every available qualifier. The costs, which are documented and substantial, are described as contained without reservation. Ostensibly the register is uniformly cautious; in practice the caution is applied asymmetrically, and it is applied to precisely the findings that would be inconvenient if stated plainly.

One might presume this is accidental. It is conceivable. Somewhat against that reading, however, the executive summary drops the qualifiers altogether, and it is the summary that will be quoted.

The caveat, then, is not really addressed to the reader. It is addressed to the future — to whoever, up to a point reasonably, might later ask why the scheme was recommended. Broadly speaking, that is what provisional language is for in documents of this kind. It is not there to describe uncertainty. It is there to be produced afterwards, in principle as evidence of care, and in practice as a receipt.

The Case and Its Joints

الحجة ومفاصلها
C1.R.3 C1.R.5

275 words

An argument fails at its joints far more often than at its premises. This is worth stating plainly, insofar as most training in critical reading concentrates on evidence, whereas the errors that survive peer review are almost invariably errors of connection.

Consider a structure common in policy writing. A premise is established; evidence is presented; a conclusion follows, introduced by "thus" or "hence" or, more ambitiously, "accordingly". The evidence may be impeccable. Nevertheless the conjunction is doing work that has not been earned, in that it asserts a relation the data do not contain. Correlation is reported and causation is inferred, whereby a marker becomes a mechanism without anybody claiming that it is one.

Conversely, a genuinely cohesive argument can be recognised by a simple test. Remove every discourse marker and read what remains. If the sequence still compels, the markers were describing a structure that was there. If it collapses into a list, they were supplying one that was not. Coherence, in other words, is a property of the reasoning; cohesion is a property of the prose, and the second is regularly mistaken for the first.

Notwithstanding this, moreover is not a crime. Furthermore, the practice of signposting is what allows a reader to follow a long chain at all, and papers stripped of it are measurably harder to use.

The rationale for attending to joints is therefore practical. Evidence can be checked by anyone with the data. A connective cannot, which is why it is where a weak case is most safely concealed, and why the reviewer who concedes the evidence and interrogates the conjunction is the one who tends to refute something.

The Extent to Which

The index, most of whom who cite it have never examined, combines nine indicators, many of which measure the same underlying quantity. This note delineates what it can and cannot support.

Begin with the parameters. Each indicator is normalised to a scale of one to ten, a step by means of which incommensurable quantities are made addable, and at which point most of the interpretive damage is done. Air quality, a continuous variable with a well-characterised threshold above which harm is documented, is thereby rendered comparable to institutional trust, for which no threshold has ever been specified. The way in which the two are then averaged implies a spectrum of equivalent magnitude. There is none.

Two consequences follow, as a result of which the index is routinely misapplied. First, movement is uninterpretable at the margin: a city may rise four places on changes in three indicators, none of which is individually significant, in which case the aggregate is precise without being accurate. Second, the criterion by which cities are grouped into bands is a quantile, not a substantive cut, whereby a difference of 0.2 becomes a difference of category.

The index does one thing well, and it is worth being explicit about it. Where the degree to which two cities differ is large — a point at which the composite comfortably exceeds its own error — the ranking is robust and has been validated against nuanced local data on four occasions.

Used comprehensively, it is a screening instrument. Used to quantify improvement year on year, it characterises noise. The distinction is not subtle, and the elaborate methodological appendix, which does specify it, is not what gets read.

281 words

Two conventions govern professional prose, and neither is a rule. The first is that repetition should be avoided; the second, that meaning should never depend on the reader's memory. The former is taught; the latter is not, and where they conflict the elliptical option usually wins.

Consider a paragraph that introduces two funding streams and then discusses "the latter" three sentences later. Such a construction is economical, and it is correct. Whether it is readable depends on something the writer cannot observe: whether the reader, at that point, still holds both items in the same order. Many do. Some do not, and if not, the sentence is not merely harder — it silently reverses.

Substitution carries the same risk in a milder form. "Do so" is admirably concise, and likewise "if so" and "the ones I mentioned"; nobody would omit them on principle. But each substitutes a short form for an antecedent that must be retrieved, and retrieval is where a tired reader fails. Vice versa, prose that refuses all substitution reads as though addressed to a machine, and is genuinely worse.

The practical test is not stylistic. Read the sentence in isolation. If it remains unambiguous, the ellipsis was efficient. If it does not, you have not been concise; you have transferred work, and implicitly asserted that the reader would rather do it than read six more words. Most would not.

Redundancy, then, is not always waste. A phrase repeated once, at the point where a reader is likely to have lost it, costs a line and buys accuracy. Writers trained to streamline everything omit it, and are surprised to be misread by people who were, in fact, reading carefully.

The Sheer Scale of It

الحجم الهائل
C1.R.4

284 words

The campaign material is remarkably consistent. The need is "utterly urgent", the current provision "nowhere near adequate", the proposal "by far the most effective option available". I do appreciate the sincerity behind this. I also notice that not one of those three phrases contains a number.

Intensification is not deception, and it would be downright unfair to suggest otherwise. Where a writer is thoroughly familiar with a subject and profoundly convinced, emphatic language is the natural register, and prose that refuses it altogether reads as evasive. Far too much professional writing is flattened by a fear of seeming committed.

But emphasis substitutes for evidence with unusual ease, and it does so all the more effectively because it is not a claim. "Considerably worse" cannot be checked. "Worse by eleven per cent" can, and is markedly less persuasive, which is decidedly the point. A reader who asks what "vastly" means is treated as pedantic, and a writer who avoids the word is thought weak. The incentives run one way.

What did happen — and this is the part the material does state plainly, once, in a footnote — is that provision fell by six per cent over four years. That is a real decline. It is distinctly less than the text implies, and it is beyond doubt enough to justify the proposal on its own.

Which is what makes the rhetoric self-defeating. The case is sound. By underscoring it with language that cannot be verified, the campaign has made a verifiable argument look like an unverifiable one, and has invited the only rebuttal that was ever available: not that the need is small, but that the people describing it were unwilling to say how large.

As It Turned Out

وكما تبيّن لاحقًا
C1.R.3

297 words

In the spring of 2008 the factory was going to close. Everyone involved understood this, and the only question was the order of events. As it turned out, the order was the whole story.

The closure had been due to take effect in September. Prior to that, in June, the company was to have transferred its two largest contracts to a plant three hundred kilometres away. Little did anyone know that the receiving plant would itself be on the point of a labour dispute, which by the time the transfer was scheduled had escalated to the extent that nothing could be moved. The factory was therefore kept open, at first for six weeks, thereafter indefinitely.

Meanwhile the workforce, which had been told in March that it would later be offered relocation, had begun to leave. Forty per cent had gone by August. What was destined to be a managed wind-down became, without anyone deciding it, an understaffed continuation, and the plant that was to become a distribution depot subsequently spent four years as neither.

In retrospect the turning point is easy to identify, and it is not the labour dispute. It is a decision taken in April, which at the outset looked purely administrative: the company chose not to announce the closure date to customers until the transfer was complete. Because customers were not told, orders continued to arrive. Because orders arrived, the six-week extension was commercially defensible. Because it was defensible, it was granted, and it was never seriously revisited.

The site is profitable today. Its survival is regularly attributed to loyalty, and occasionally to foresight. Neither is quite it. It was bound to close, and it did not, because a communications decision made in April was allowed to transpire into a strategy nobody had chosen.

On Condition That

بشرط أن
C1.R.3 C1.R.5

288 words

A grant agreement is a machine for allocating uncertainty, and it works only insofar as every condition in it is attached to somebody who can satisfy it. Provided that this holds, the document is a genuine allocation of risk. Where it does not, the risk has not disappeared; it has simply been left where it fell.

Consider a clause of a familiar type. Funds are released on condition that construction begins within twelve months, unless delay is attributable to regulatory approval, in which case the period is extended. Supposing the approval takes fourteen months — as it did — the extension is triggered and nobody is at fault. So far the machine works.

Now consider what the agreement did not stipulate. Assuming, as both parties evidently did, that the contractor's price would hold for those fourteen months, no provision was made for the eventuality that it would not. But for that omission, the escalation would have been a shared contingency. As long as it went unmentioned, it remained wholly with the recipient, who had no means of hedging it and no standing to renegotiate.

This is the general form of the problem. A condition that is written is a condition that has been priced by somebody. A prerequisite that is merely presupposed is subject to no such discipline, and it will hinge on whichever party is least able to walk away.

Given that drafting time is finite, the practical counsel is narrow: for every assumption on which the schedule depends, ask who bears it if it fails. Were the answer to be "the weaker party, by default", the clause has not been drafted. It has been declined, and the reciprocal obligation everyone believes they signed does not exist.

A Can of Worms

فتح باب لا يُغلق
C1.R.4

292 words

The minutes record that the committee agreed the matter was "a grey area" and resolved not to "open a can of worms". Both phrases are doing something more specific than they appear to, and it is worth getting to the bottom of what.

A grey area, taken literally, is a region where a rule does not determine an outcome. Used figuratively, and it is nearly always used figuratively, it more often describes a region where the rule is perfectly clear and applying it would be costly. The connotation is of intellectual difficulty. The function is permission to stop. That is a double-edged sword: the phrase is genuinely useful where ambiguity is real, and it is precisely because it is useful that it works so well when it is not.

"A can of worms" is more candid, in a way. It concedes that further enquiry would find something. The undertone is not that the question is hard but that the answer would be inconvenient, and the metaphor is chosen — one may infer — by people who would rather not be quoted saying so.

By and large this is how such expressions earn their place. They evoke a shared attitude while committing nobody to a proposition. In the long run they also shape what a committee is able to notice, since a matter filed as grey is not filed as unresolved, and only one of those two categories generates a review date.

None of which is an argument for banning idiom, an allusion-free prose being both unreadable and no more honest. It is an argument for reading the minutes figuratively. What was actually decided here was not to look, and the phrase that shed light on it is the one nobody drafted carefully.

We Regret to Inform You

يأسفنا أن نبلغكم
C1.R.4

290 words

Two documents describe the same decision. The first is the letter that was sent. The second is the email a manager wrote to her own team an hour earlier, which was later disclosed. Reading them together is the most efficient lesson in register I know.

The letter commences: "Please be advised that, with respect to your application, we regret to inform you that the panel has been unable to proceed on this occasion." It requires eighteen words to say no. It attributes the decision to a panel, which is courteous and also true in a limited sense, and it uses "unable", which implies constraint where a choice was in fact made. Nothing in it is false. Its formality is not decoration; it is doing the work of obscuring agency.

The email reads: "We're not going ahead. It's not the proposal, it's that we've got loads of these and only two slots, and honestly the other lot know the sector better. Tell folks it was competitive, which it was." That is blunt, colloquial, and considerably more informative. It supplies the actual criterion, the comparison, and a suggested public formulation, and it would be indefensible to send to an applicant.

The interesting question is not which is honest. Both are. The letter conceals who decided and why; the email conceals nothing and would humiliate the reader. Register is not a scale from evasive to plain. It is a choice about which information to purchase and what to pay for it.

What a professional writer should be able to do is name that price. To paraphrase the letter accurately, one has to obtain something it declines to state — and knowing that a euphemism is present is a different skill from resenting it.

A Growing Body of Evidence

حجم متزايد من الأدلة
C1.R.5 C1.R.3

280 words

Consider the phrase "the well-documented long-term impact of export-driven, labour-intensive growth on deeply rooted regional inequality". Twelve words, one head noun, and at least four claims that are not being defended.

Dense premodification is the characteristic register of the policy literature, and its economy of expression is real. To condense that phrase into clauses would take three sentences, and a reader who works in the field will encapsulate it correctly without effort. Precision of a kind is achieved: the writer has specified which impact, of which growth, on which inequality.

What is lost is the distinction between what is established and what is asserted. "Well-documented" is a modifier, so it is not a proposition and cannot be challenged as one. A significant proportion of the increasingly complex apparatus of this genre works the same way. A widely held belief becomes an adjective; a contested causal mechanism becomes a compound premodifier; and "state-of-the-art" or "purpose-built" or "cutting-edge" arrive as description rather than evaluation, having been evaluated by nobody in particular.

The effect compounds. Because the head noun is late, the reader assembles the qualifications first and meets the subject already framed. The extent of the damage is difficult to quantify — which is itself a sentence of this type, and one I would not be able to substantiate if pressed.

There is no remedy in shorter sentences, streamlined prose being perfectly capable of the same manoeuvre. The remedy is a reading habit: unpack the noun phrase into the claims it contains, and ask of each which is evidence and which is furniture. In this literature the underlying causes of disagreement are usually sitting inside a modifier, where they cannot be cited.

Or Rather, Let Me Put It Another Way

أو بالأحرى، خليني أقولها بطريقة تانية
C1.R.3 C1.R.4

288 words

There is a persistent belief among advanced learners that fluency means never stopping. The point I'm making is close to the opposite, and it is worth elaborating rather than asserting.

Consider what a proficient speaker actually does. They signpost — "to return to your first question" — which allows a listener to survive a digression of thirty seconds without losing the thread. They correct themselves openly: "the policy was introduced in 2011, or rather it was drafted in 2011 and introduced two years later." And having said that, they recap at intervals, not because they have forgotten but because they can hear that the listener might have.

None of this is smooth. Transcribed, it looks repetitive. What it is, is coherent, and coherence is what the descriptors reward. Spontaneity without signposting produces speech that is easy to produce and hard to follow, which is the failure mode of the confident learner. Long-winded is not the opposite of succinct; both can be perfectly coherent, and both can fail.

To sum up — and in a nutshell this is the whole argument — the ability to hold the floor is not the ability to keep talking. It is the ability to keep a listener oriented while you think. Paraphrase, clarify, digress and consolidate: these are load-bearing, and a speaker who has none of them is not fluent but merely quick.

What I mean is this, and bear with me because it sounds like a paradox. The most eloquent people you know are the ones you have most often heard say "let me put it another way". They are not struggling. They have simply noticed something you did not say, and they are the only kind of speaker who ever does.
