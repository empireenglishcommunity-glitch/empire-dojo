So Be It

فليكن
C2.R.2

300 words

It is essential that a drafter know the difference between a wish and a provision, and it is imperative that the reader be able to tell which he has been handed. Be that as it may, the mandative subjunctive has become so formulaic that it now conceals precisely the distinction it once marked.

Suffice it to say that 'the committee recommend that the scheme proceed' and 'the committee require that the scheme proceed' are, in the syntax, identical. Both take the bare subjunctive; both would be indistinguishable were the verbs exchanged. Yet one is advisable and the other binding, and the whole weight of the sentence rests on a lexical choice the form does nothing to mark. Far be it from me to propose that we ordain a new grammar. The remedy is duller: that drafters stipulate, in a decree's opening provision, which of their verbs carry obligation and which do not.

Consider what happens when they do not. A reader encountering 'it is vital that the record be retained' cannot know whether retention is a duty or a counsel of prudence, and lest that seem a small thing, it is the difference between a breach and a preference. God forbid the question ever reaches a tribunal, where the solemn decorum of the construction will be read as evidence of intent — the form is grave, therefore the obligation was meant to bind.

That inference is unwarranted, and it is made daily. Long live the subjunctive, by all means; it is one of the few genuinely economical things left in formal English. But henceforth, come what may, let the verb do the binding and not the mood. Otherwise the most solemn sentence in the document will also be its least determinate, which is a peculiar way to write a rule.

But for a Single Line

لولا سطر واحد
C2.R.2

298 words

Were it not for a supposition nobody troubled to test, the eastern network would have been commissioned on schedule. That is the counterfactual on which the entire inquiry has been predicated, and it is worth asking whether it can bear the weight.

Had it not been for the omission of a right of way from the 2019 tender, the contingency would have been priced. But for that omission, the delay was conceivable rather than inevitable, and every subsequent eventuality — machinery routed nine kilometres about, a trench unopened before the rains, pumping stations commissioned out of sequence — hinges upon it. Should the need arise to apportion blame, the line is short and it is clear.

And yet. Were she to be asked, the lead engineer would say, as she said at the time, that a project of this scale forestalls nothing by listing risks it cannot avert; that the reprieve everyone imagines was contingent upon a municipality behaving as it had never behaved; and that a document which anticipated every dire eventuality would be a document nobody read.

Save for one thing, she would be right. The omitted item was not a risk in the ordinary sense but a prerequisite — something the schedule was predicated on and which lay outside the contractor's control. Hypothetically, a prerequisite can be insured; a hazard can only be reserved against. The distinction is not academic, and had they known it, the reviewers would have looked for the provision rather than the reserve.

What precipitated the downfall, then, was neither negligence nor bad luck. It was a taxonomy: the thing was filed as a contingency, and contingencies are priced, whereas prerequisites are secured. Providential, in a sense, that it failed so publicly. A quieter failure would have taught nobody the difference.

This Much Is Clear

ده على الأقل واضح
C2.R.2

292 words

What strikes me, reading a hundred of these a week, is how rarely the sentence ends where the argument does. That much is clear from any sample: the finding sits in the middle, and the qualification takes the end-focus.

Of particular note is what this does to a reader. End-weight is not decoration; it is where attention settles, and a writer who puts the caveat last has made the caveat the theme whether or not that was the intention. One thing is certain — the reader will carry away the final clause. This I must say plainly, because it is treated as a matter of taste and it is not. It is a matter of what gets remembered.

Consider two arrangements of one finding. 'The intervention improved outcomes, although the sample was small.' And: 'Although the sample was small, the intervention improved outcomes.' The propositional content is identical. The salient element is not, and in the second the improvement is foregrounded while the limitation is subordinate. Neither is dishonest. But a reader skimming forty abstracts will retain the first as a caveat and the second as a result, and both writers will believe they reported the same thing.

What follows is not a rule but a discipline: decide which element you want prominent, and arrange the sentence so that it arrives last. Downplay by subordinating, prioritise by placing. Juxtapose the two versions before you submit, and if they read differently to you, they will read differently to a committee.

The orderly version of this advice is duller than the phenomenon deserves. Deliberate sequencing is the cheapest instrument of emphasis in the language, and the only one that costs nothing and signals nothing. It is also, for that reason, the one nobody checks.

What It Comes Down To

It is not the evidence that persuades a committee; it is the sentence the evidence arrives in. What most drafters do is assemble the facts and trust them, and the thing that matters is precisely what they leave to chance.

Every cleft carries a presupposition, and the presupposition is where the work is done. 'It was the delay that caused the overspend' does not argue that there was an overspend; it assumes one, and invites the reader to dispute only the cause. All the sentence has conceded is the part nobody now examines. That is the hallmark of the construction and, one might add, its whole utility: the crux is smuggled into the frame.

The reason this is worth naming is that the technique is invisible in exactly the way a good technique is. What a reader does is arrive at the contrast the writer has set up — delay, or something else — and the place the argument is won is not there at all. It is in the clause that established the overspend as given. There is a discipline that pinpoints this, and it consists of one question: what has this sentence assumed in order to ask its question?

It is not until you apply that test to your own drafting that its reach becomes uncomfortable. The essence of most contested paragraphs I have read is a presupposition nobody chose deliberately. What single them out is that they survive review — the nub of the disagreement is never in the disputed clause, and reviewers, being trained on claims, attend to claims.

The linchpin, then, is not honesty. It is noticing. A writer who clefts on purpose has decided what to make undisputed, and can be asked to defend it. A writer who clefts by habit has made that decision anyway.

The Onset of Everything

The proliferation of abstraction in institutional prose is not a stylistic misfortune but a functional adaptation, and its ramifications are systemic. Grammatical metaphor — the encapsulation of a process as a thing — permits a density no clausal arrangement can match, and the compression is genuine.

Observe what the transformation costs. 'The erosion of confidence' occasions no question about who lost confidence in what, whereas 'people stopped trusting the agency' occasions both. The advent of the abstract noun brings about the disappearance of the participant. What is attributable to whom becomes unaskable, not because anybody concealed it but because the syntax no longer has a slot for it.

The cumulative trajectory is towards a prose in which phenomena occur without agents. The deterioration in standards, the curtailment of provision, the imposition of limits, the alleviation of harm: each of these is a nominal encapsulation of something somebody did, and the consequent text can describe an entire administrative catastrophe without naming a single actor. That is not a conspiracy. It is what the resource affords, and writers reach for what is afforded.

Two ramifications follow. The first is diagnostic: an abstract-heavy paragraph is a place to look for a missing participant, and the question 'who?' applied to each nominalisation will usually engender either an answer or an embarrassment. The second is more troubling. Because the density is real, the prose is efficient for those already inside the field, whose reading supplies the participants automatically from inception. The register therefore does not fail — it works, for insiders, which is precisely why it survives and precipitates its own reproduction.

The underlying phenomenon, then, is not obscurity. It is a form of writing whose comprehensibility is inversely related to how much you need explained.

As Noted Above

As will be shown, the apparatus of signposting in academic prose does two jobs, and it is worth stressing at the outset that only one of them is acknowledged.

The first is navigational and uncontroversial. It should be borne in mind that a reader entering a forty-page argument has no map, and phrases such as 'as noted above', 'to put it briefly' and 'as we shall see' constitute one. By way of illustration: strip the meta-discourse from a long methods section and comprehension of the individual claims is unaffected while recall of the throughline collapses. The framework is doing real work.

The second job is less innocent. To digress briefly — though the digression is the point — a signpost asserts a relation as well as marking one. 'As noted above' presupposes that the thing was noted, and that it was noted in the sense now being relied upon. Needless to say, those are different claims, and the second is rarely checked. In parenthesis: I have twice found 'as demonstrated earlier' pointing at a paragraph that raised a possibility and demonstrated nothing, and in neither case had a reviewer queried it.

That said, the remedy is not less signposting. Returning to the earlier point, a text without a thread is worse. The proviso is narrower: a signpost is a claim about your own text, and it should be verifiable in the same way as any other. Recapitulate what you actually established, not what you meant to.

To delineate the caveat precisely, and then I will expound no further: the danger is not that readers are misled by 'as noted above'. It is that writers are. A discursive habit that foreshadows coherence will, given a succinct enough summary, persuade its author that the coherence was achieved.

On the Face of It

By all accounts the valve was closed. It stands to reason that it was: the procedure requires it, the operator is experienced, and to the best of my knowledge no one has suggested otherwise. On the face of it, then, the matter is settled.

It is not, and the reason is that every sentence in that paragraph reports a different kind of warrant. 'By all accounts' is testimony. 'It stands to reason' is inference from the general to the particular, which is the weakest of the three and the most persuasive. 'To the best of my knowledge' is an absence of contrary evidence, which is not evidence. Presumably the reader aggregates them into a single impression of likelihood, and in all likelihood that impression is stronger than any of the components warrants.

If anything, the epistemic vocabulary of English is too rich here rather than too poor. Purportedly, ostensibly, seemingly, evidently, demonstrably — the last of these is a claim about proof and the other four are claims about appearance, and they arrive in the same syntactic slot with the same unremarkable rhythm. As far as I can tell, no reader distinguishes them reliably at speed, and I include myself.

What is tenable, then? Only this: that a conjecture may be plausible, that a surmise may be warranted, and that neither is verifiable until somebody has attested to something specific. To my mind the test is procedural rather than logical. Postulate the opposite and ask what would have to be found. If the answer is 'a record', the claim is speculative until the record is produced.

All the evidence suggests we are poor at this. There is reason to believe we are poorest precisely where the stakes make care most necessary, which is where the vocabulary is thickest.

The Word You Wanted

A misnomer is not a small thing. To refute a claim is to disprove it; to rebut is to answer it; to contest is to declare it open; to dispute is to argue with it. Only the first asserts that the matter is closed, and it is the one used when none of the others will do the rhetorical work.

The same collapse afflicts quantity. Substantial denotes size, considerable denotes enough size to matter, and significant — outside statistics — has been worn down to mean roughly 'quite big', which is a pity, because inside statistics it means something precise and the two now share a page. A reader encountering 'a significant reduction' cannot always tell which is meant, and the felicitous choice would be to say which.

Terminate, conclude, cease. The first is transitive and administrative, the second implies completion, the third describes stopping without implying that an end was reached. A contract that has ceased has not necessarily concluded, and an employee whose post is terminated has not concluded anything at all. These are not shades a careful reader can ignore; they are the difference between a description and a claim.

Consider the connotative cluster around silence. Keen, avid and eager differ in warmth; reticent, taciturn and laconic differ in what they attribute. Reticent is a reluctance to disclose, and it is about a topic. Taciturn is a disposition, and it is about a person. Laconic is a style, and it is a compliment. To call a witness taciturn where you mean reticent is to convert a choice into a character.

None of this is pedantry, and the test is easy: if substituting the near-synonym changes what a reader would then be entitled to infer, the words were never interchangeable and the precise one was doing work.

The new director is frugal. Her predecessor was stingy. As far as anyone can establish, they operate identical expenses policies.

This is not hypocrisy; it is vocabulary, and it is worth being precise about how it works. Frugal and stingy denote the same behaviour and connote opposite evaluations — the first is complimentary, the second pejorative, and a writer choosing between them has already delivered a verdict while appearing to report. Thrifty sits between, faintly approving and faintly dated.

The pairs are everywhere once noticed. Assertive and pushy. Slim and skinny. Childlike and childish, where the same directness is extolled in one and belittled in the other. A regime, incidentally, is what other people's arrangements are called; ours are policies.

What makes this hard to police is that neither word is false. Nobody can be corrected for writing 'stingy' the way they can for writing a wrong date. The slant is carried in the overtone, which is deniable by construction, and a writer accused of bias can retreat to the denotation with perfect honesty: I merely said she does not spend much.

The euphemism and its opposite are the same instrument pointed in different directions. To laud is to insinuate merit; to denigrate is to insinuate its absence; and the neutral term, where one exists, is usually clumsy, which is why it loses. 'She spends less than the budget permits' is accurate, damning of nobody, and nine words long.

A modest proposal, then, for anyone drafting a profile. Choose the flattering word or the damning one, but notice that you have chosen. The one thing not available is the belief that you have simply described her.

It is also, one has to concede, sometimes a coward's instrument. 'Hardly ideal' permits a manager to register serious criticism while retaining the option of having meant it mildly, and if challenged he may deflate the whole thing — banter, surely — leaving the recipient to look sardonic for having noticed. Irony is double-edged in precisely that way: it insinuates while preserving deniability, and the deniability is the appeal.

'It's just a scratch,' says the man with the severed arm, and we laugh because the gap between the words and the world is doing the work. Wry, mock-modest, facetious: the family is large, and its members are not interchangeable. Getting one wrong is not a small error either, though I would not call it a catastrophe.

The first half is elevated to the point of parody — incumbent, present author, foregoing — and the second is plain-spoken to the point of cliché. The juxtaposition is jarring, deliberately, and if you found it faintly comic then the bathos worked; if you found it merely clumsy, that is the risk one runs.

Register is not a dial from formal to casual. It is a set of promises about the relationship, and mixing registers breaks one of them, which is why it is funny when controlled and merely incongruous when not. Mock-formal diction — the highfalutin word deployed about something trivial — is the oldest reliable joke in English, and it works because gravitas is a costume. Put it on a small subject and the costume is visible.

The failure mode is instructive. A writer who modulates well is audience-aware: she calibrates the levity against what the reader can bear, tempers the flippancy where the subject is raw, and the seams do not show. A writer who modulates badly produces something worse than uniformity, because the reader cannot tell which register is the sincere one. Irreverence about a redundancy programme does not read as deft. It reads as a man who has not understood what he is discussing.

Which brings us, with a deftness I shall let the reader assess, to the counterpoint. The memorandum was not badly written. It was written in one register throughout, and that register was wrong, and one uniform wrong note resonates a great deal longer than several right ones.

The Consummate Thing

Mastery is the most oversold word in language teaching, and I have sold it myself, so let me try to say what it is not.

It is not range. A learner with an enormous vocabulary and no sense of occasion is not accomplished; he is equipped. It is not precision either, though precision is nearer. The most precise speaker I ever taught was incomprehensible at parties, because he brought the same finesse to a joke that he brought to a specification, and finesse in the wrong register is just conspicuousness.

What consummate command looks like, as far as I can tell, is dexterity in the choosing. The versatility to hear what a situation wants — brevity here, elaboration there, and in a surprising number of cases nothing at all — and then to do that, seamlessly, without the doing being visible. Which means mastery is mostly invisible, and therefore mostly unrewarded, and therefore not what anybody trains for.

There is a hallmark, though, and I would offer it as the capstone of everything in this course. An assured speaker is one who can be brief without seeming curt, elaborate without seeming long-winded, and — this is the rare one — silent without seeming absent. Those three are not separate skills. They are one skill, which is the integration of everything else, and it is the culmination of a great deal of orchestration nobody sees.

So the authoritative version of the advice is disappointing. Consolidate. Adapt. Notice what the room needs. And accept that the more polished you become, the less anyone will remark on it, because the distinguished quality of really accomplished English is that it draws no attention to itself at all.

Which is, I concede, a difficult thing to put on a certificate.
